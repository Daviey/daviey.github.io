CISO: The quantum deadline moved.

Sysadmin: The 2035 one?

CISO: June's executive order. 2030 for key exchange, 2031 for signatures.

Sysadmin: That's four years out.

CISO: The inventory takes two if you start today. Ask the firmware.

Sysadmin: The firmware is from 2019 and the vendor got acquired twice since.

CISO: Then it stays on the list forever. Welcome to post-quantum migration.

On 22 June 2026, Executive Order 14412 turned post-quantum cryptography from a conference track into a compliance date. US federal agencies must be on quantum-resistant key establishment by 31 December 2030 and quantum-resistant signatures by 31 December 2031. Through the Federal Acquisition Regulation Council the same dates land on every covered contractor, which in practice means most of the supply chain. Yesterday the IETF published RFC 10024, putting the hybrid X25519MLKEM768 key exchange on the standards track for TLS 1.3. The browsers already have it on by default. Cloudflare already serves it.

Your government and your browser vendor agree on the message. Stop planning, start shipping.

"A quantum computer doesn't have to exist for the attack to work. Recording your traffic today and filing it away is free."

I run my own CA, a fleet of Linux boxes, and enough SSH to constitute a lifestyle. This post is the migration order I'm actually following: what the algorithms are, what you can turn on this week, where the real time sink hides, and why signatures will hurt more than key exchange.

Why Now: Deadlines, Not Predictions

Nobody sane claims a cryptographically useful quantum computer arrives next year. The deadline doesn't care, because the attack that motivates it works today: harvest now, decrypt later. An adversary records your TLS sessions in 2026, stores the bytes, and decrypts them the year a suitable machine exists. Anything confidential past that horizon, medical records, state archives, signing root material, signed contracts, is already exposed in transit. The OpenSSL Foundation's analysis of the order and the news coverage both land on the same reading: the 2030 date is not a prediction about hardware, it's a buffer about logistics.

The logistics are the story. Enterprise crypto migrations consistently take longer than the cryptography warrants, and Google and Microsoft have both pulled their internal completion targets forward to 2029 to stay ahead of the federal dates. Surveys of migration programs keep finding the same long pole: finding every deployment of vulnerable crypto takes 12 to 24 months before you change a single key, and full estate migrations are measured in five to fifteen years. Four years to 2030 is not four years of slack. It's roughly one inventory phase plus one rollout, executed without waiting for anyone's approval process.

The Algorithms You Actually Need

Three NIST standards went final in August 2024, and one more is coming:

  • FIPS 203, ML-KEM. The key encapsulation mechanism. Replaces the RSA and ECDH maths inside key exchange. ML-KEM-768 is the workhorse for TLS.
  • FIPS 204, ML-DSA. The primary signature scheme, for certificates, code signing, and documents.
  • FIPS 205, SLH-DSA. A stateless hash-based backup signature scheme. Slower and bigger, but its security story is the most conservative in the set.
  • FIPS 206, FN-DSA. Still draft, expected final between late 2026 and early 2027. Don't build around it yet.

You will not deploy any of them raw. You will deploy hybrids. The one worth internalising is X25519MLKEM768, which runs classical X25519 and ML-KEM-768 together and derives the session key from both. The handshake stays secret unless the attacker breaks both constructions. Against today's attackers that is exactly as strong as X25519, and it costs one extra kilobyte and change of handshake. RFC 10024 formalised exactly this pattern for TLS 1.3. There is no compatibility cliff and no performance argument. Which raises the question of why anyone is still waiting.

The Easy Win: Hybrid Key Exchange Everywhere

Start with key exchange because it's the urgent half (harvest now, decrypt later only touches data in transit) and because it's genuinely cheap.

TLS: check, then flip

OpenSSL 3.5 and every major stack negotiate X25519MLKEM768 automatically when both ends offer it. Find out where you stand:

$ openssl s_client -connect example.com:443 -tls1_3 -brief </dev/null
...
CONNECTION ESTABLISHED
Protocol version: TLSv1.3
Ciphersuite: TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384
Peer signature type: ECDSA
Server Temp Key: X25519MLKEM768

Server Temp Key is the answer. If it says plain X25519 or P-256, one of the two ends predates hybrid support and the handshake fell back.

On your own servers, put the hybrid group first and keep the classical one as fallback so ancient clients degrade instead of dying:

# nginx against OpenSSL 3.5+
ssl_ecdh_curve X25519MLKEM768:X25519;

Your public edge may already be done without your help. The big CDNs terminated hybrid by default months ago, which upgraded half the internet in passing. The edges you control yourself, load balancers, mail servers, internal APIs, database listeners, are the ones to walk by hand.

SSH: one line, one log entry

OpenSSH has shipped the hybrid mlkem768x25519-sha256 exchange since 9.9 in September 2024. Same pattern: list it first, keep the fallback.

# /etc/ssh/sshd_config
KexAlgorithms mlkem768x25519-sha256,curve25519-sha256

Verify from the client side where the negotiation is visible:

$ ssh -v host 2>&1 | grep 'kex: algorithm'
debug1: kex: algorithm: mlkem768x25519-sha256

And audit what your estate actually offers, because defaults vary by distro and by however long that appliance image has been baked:

$ nmap -p 22 --script ssh2-enum-algos bastion.internal
...
|  kex_algorithms: (4)
|      curve25519-sha256
|      ecdh-sha2-nistp256
|      diffie-hellman-group14-sha256
|      diffie-hellman-group-exchange-sha256

That output is a finding. Four algorithms, all classical, on the box everyone jumps through.

Nothing teaches humility like your own inventory. The newest box in my fleet, imaged this spring from a current distro, offered four classical key exchanges and no strict KEX, because defaults move slower than releases and nobody asks a fresh install to justify itself. A box nobody thinks about is still a key exchange deployment. Everything with a socket is on the list. The SSH hardening post covers the rest of that checklist.

The Long Pole: Crypto Inventory

The order says migrate. Your first deliverable is a list, because you cannot migrate what you cannot find, and crypto hides better than almost anything else in an estate. The industry name for the artefact is a cryptographic bill of materials, CBOM, and it's the SBOM's boring sibling. One row per key and certificate: who owns it, what algorithm and size, where the private key lives, who rotated it last, and what breaks the day you change it.

Places crypto actually hides, from experience:

  • Certificates on disk that no scanner owns: management planes, IPMI, printer TLS, that S3-compatible appliance in the corner
  • Firmware signatures verified by boot ROMs that only accept RSA-2048, the 2019 vendor from the dialogue
  • Long-lived secrets in backups: encrypted archive formats sealed with keys nobody has rolled since the format shipped
  • Application-level crypto: JWT signing keys, session cookies, password reset tokens, Fernet keys in config files
  • Third-party tunnels: vendor VPN concentrators, support shadow accesses, the MCP server an engineer wired up in June
  • Your own CA and everything it signed, which is its own section below

Discovery is not one tool, it's a small toolkit run repeatedly:

# every cert on every box, with expiry and algorithm
find / -name '*.pem' -o -name '*.crt' 2>/dev/null \
  | xargs -r -n1 openssl x509 -noout -subject -enddate -text 2>/dev/null \
  | grep -E 'subject=|notAfter=|Public Key Algorithm'

# every private key your filesystem is protecting badly
find / -name '*.key' -o -name '*id_rsa*' 2>/dev/null

# what SSH estate offers, fleet-wide
nmap -p 22 --script ssh2-enum-algos 10.0.0.0/8

# what TLS estate negotiates, fleet-wide (feed it your asset list)
while read host; do
  openssl s_client -connect "$host:443" -tls1_3 </dev/null 2>/dev/null \
    | grep 'Server Temp Key' | sed "s|^|$host |"
done < targets.txt

None of this finds the firmware verifier or the backup archive sealed in 2021. Those surface from the asset inventory and vendor questionnaires, which is precisely why the inventory phase eats 12 to 24 months: the tools take a week, the phone calls take a year.

Signatures: The Half That Breaks Protocols

Key exchange is cheap because a KEM fits where the old maths sat. Signatures are the opposite: ML-DSA is large enough to break assumptions baked into protocols and provisioning formats.

SchemePublic keySignature
Ed2551932 B64 B
ECDSA P-25664 B~72 B
RSA-2048270 B256 B
ML-DSA-441,312 B2,420 B
ML-DSA-651,952 B3,309 B
ML-DSA-872,592 B4,627 B

An ML-DSA-65 signature is fifty times the size of Ed25519's, and certificates carry both signature and public key. A typical three-cert chain goes from a couple of kilobytes to something like twenty. TLS handshakes can absorb that on fast links, though embedded clients with fixed buffers cannot, and that's the mild case. Firmware update slots sized in 2015 for RSA-2048 do not fit ML-DSA at all. Smart cards, TPMs, HSMs and secure elements have ML-DSA support arriving on their own schedules, generally "later". Document signing formats balloon. Certificate transparency logs and OCSP responses grow with them.

This is why the order gives signatures until 2031 and why FIPS 206 stalling until 2027 barely matters yet. Nobody should be rushing ML-DSA into constrained environments this year. The 2031 work that starts now is the unglamorous kind: sizing, capacity planning, vendor roadmaps in writing, and the internal PKI plan.

Internal PKI Gets a Second Root

I wrote previously about building a CA with guardrails and running step-ca. Internal PKI migrations converge on the same shape:

  1. Hybrid key exchange now. Everything your CA's certificates protect, TLS and SSH both, flips to hybrid groups. The certificates themselves stay ECDSA or RSA; key exchange and authentication are separable and you migrate them at different speeds.
  2. A parallel ML-DSA test root. New root, ML-DSA-65, cross-signed by nothing, issued to every service that volunteers. Nothing production-critical trusts it. Its job is to find out which clients, libraries, and monitoring stacks choke on big chains, while the answer is cheap.
  3. Shorter leaf lifetimes before the migration, not during. If your leaves live 90 days, you can re-issue the estate on a new chain in one quarter with no long-tail stragglers. The step-ca post covers automating exactly that kind of turnover.
  4. One cutoff date for classical-only issuance, publicly declared, so the stragglers have a deadline that isn't yours.

External CAs will carry the certificate size problem for you eventually; the browser-accepted ML-DSA chains are a matter of when. Internal PKI is where the size shock lands on your own formats, so the test root exists to make the shock routine.

What To Do On Monday

  1. Run the two fleet checks, ssh2-enum-algos and the TLS temp-key sweep, and count the classical-only endpoints. That count is your inventory seed and your progress metric.
  2. Flip hybrid key exchange on everywhere you control an endpoint: nginx groups, sshd_config, HAProxy, Postgres, whatever terminates TLS. Fallbacks keep it break-safe.
  3. Check backup retention against 2030. Anything encrypted in transit today and retained past 2030 is already in scope, and the archive you can't re-encrypt is your hardest asset.
  4. Write to every appliance and firmware vendor for a dated PQC roadmap. "On our roadmap" is a no. Dates or findings.
  5. Start the CBOM as a spreadsheet if you must. Its existence matters more than its schema; the NIST and RFC links below cover the format side when someone insists.
  6. Stand up the ML-DSA test root if you run internal PKI. One weekend, and it converts an unknown into a backlog.
