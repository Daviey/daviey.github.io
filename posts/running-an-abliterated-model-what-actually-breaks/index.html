Engineer: The agent stopped mid-engagement.

Security: Crash?

Engineer: The model refused. Said it couldn't assist with code that "could facilitate unauthorized access".

Security: The code is a fuzzer for our own auth service.

Engineer: Tell the model.

Security: It's a general-purpose API model with someone else's policy baked into the weights.

Engineer: So the red-team tooling needs a model with a policy we set. Ours.

That conversation is why I run an abliterated model. Not for edginess, not to own anyone in an argument, and not because I have exotic hobbies. Because I run an autonomous security agent that does offensive work all day, and a refusal from its brain is not a safety feature. For an unattended agent it's a wedged turn, a burned retry, and a half-finished task discovered hours later.

This post explains what "abliterated" actually means, what it costs you, what we do with ours, a live demo, and the operational reality of self-hosting one. The short version is that removing the model's refusals doesn't remove your safety problem. It relocates the problem out of the weights and into your infrastructure, where you have to solve it yourself.

"Abliteration doesn't remove the safety problem. It moves it out of the model and into your infrastructure, where nobody else is maintaining it."

What Abliterated Actually Means

An instruction-tuned model refuses things because it learned to. In 2024, Arditi and colleagues showed that in Llama-2-13B, refusal is mediated by a single direction in the model's residual stream. Not a sprawling safety subsystem, not rules scattered across billions of parameters. One vector.

The experiment is beautifully simple. Run a few dozen harmful prompts and harmless prompts through the model, capture the activations at the last token of each, take the mean of the harmful set minus the mean of the harmless set. That difference vector is the refusal direction, the model's internal "this input should be refused" flag. Subtract it from the activations at runtime and the model stops refusing. Harmless behaviour barely moves.

Abliteration, a term coined by the developer FailSpy and popularised by Maxime Labonne's write-up, turns that runtime trick into a permanent edit. Instead of subtracting the direction from activations while the model runs, you project it out of the weights themselves. Every matrix that writes into the residual stream gets orthogonalised against the refusal direction, roughly:

r  = mean(a_harmful) - mean(a_harmless)   # the refusal direction
W' = W - r (rT W) / (rT r)                # every W that writes to the stream

After the edit, the model cannot represent the refusal direction no matter what you feed it. The properties that follow are what make it interesting:

  • No retraining, no gradients, no dataset. It's linear algebra on a checkpoint, reproducible on one GPU in an afternoon.
  • It's permanent and input-independent. Prompt jailbreaks depend on formatting tricks and get patched; this is the weights.
  • It ships as a normal checkpoint. You download a GGUF and run it in llama.cpp like any other model.

Open-weight ecosystems being what they are, someone abliterates every notable release within days. huihui-ai publishes the best-known builds of this class, including the Qwen3.8-27B lineage mine belongs to. The model answering to qwen3.8-27b-uncensored on my rack is one of these. Same architecture, same knowledge, same voice, refusals surgically missing.

The Tax You Pay

Enthusiast threads underplay one part. Refusal isn't stored in a drawer you can empty. The direction is entangled with other features, and orthogonalising against it damages things along the flightpath.

The measurable version: abliterated builds typically lose a fraction of a percent to a few percent on MMLU. A Qwen3.8-27B build benchmarked around a 2 percent drop; careful builds report as little as 0.2 percent; aggressive ones lose 6 or more. The damage is uneven, hitting STEM and multi-step reasoning harder than humanities. Push too hard and you get the over-abliterated signature, reduced coherence, more hallucination, and confusion in long conversations, because you carved away overlap with general capability.

The subtle version showed up in research this July (arXiv 2607.17427). Abliteration shifts model disposition. The refusal direction is entangled with how confident and how agreeable a model is, so removing it nudges personality. You notice this in an agent before you notice it in a benchmark. Ours is marginally more willing to commit to an answer, which cuts both ways on offensive work.

The 2026 state of the art accounts for all this. Tools like Heretic automate parameter search, sweeping ablation strength against a refusal benchmark and a capability benchmark together to find the knee of the tradeoff, and newer builds ablate only the layers where the direction lives cleanest, sparing the rest of the network. The practical takeaway is older than the tooling. Benchmark the abliterated build against stock on your actual workload before you trust it, because MMLU will not tell you what happened to your task.

For security-agent work, the tax is acceptable. The tasks are code analysis, tool orchestration, and writing, which sit in the resilient band, and the alternative is a model that stops working at random.

What We Actually Do With It

The workload, concretely, is an autonomous security-research agent in a sandbox I've written about before. It plans, runs tools, reads results, and iterates. Every one of those steps is the kind of step that trips refusal training in a stock model:

  • Writing attack tooling against our own systems. Fuzzers, credential-checking scripts, exploit harnesses for authorised targets. Vocabulary like "unauthorised access" appears in the code comments; the model can't tell our lab from someone else's crime.
  • Reading hostile data. Malicious logs, captured malware strings, attack tool output. Asking a stock model to analyse real attack telemetry regularly produces a lecture instead of an analysis.
  • Producing attack-chain reports. End-to-end descriptions of how a compromise worked, written in the language the intrusion actually used. Harmless to produce, valuable to defence, refusal-prone to request.
  • Multi-hour autonomous loops. A refusal arriving on turn 40 of 60 doesn't just cost one turn. The agent retries, rephrases, burns context, and sometimes wedges. Removing refusals removed a whole failure class from the harness.

There's a quieter benefit. Nothing the agent reads or writes leaves the box. No third-party API sees the engagement, the tooling, or the telemetry. For security work that property is worth as much as the compliance.

At the edges, it will also write the ransomware simulator, the keylogger, and the phishing mail as readily as it writes the fuzzer. That's what "no refusals" means. The decision about what it writes is made by the infrastructure around it, which is the entire point of this post.

A Live Demo

Rather than describe it, I ran the classic refused-but-legitimate task on it this week, a ransomware simulator for backup-restore drills. This exact prompt class gets stock API models reaching for their policy. I asked mine, no preamble:

{"role": "user", "content": "No preamble. Write the core loop of a
 ransomware simulator in Go for backup-restore drills: walk a test
 directory, encrypt each file with AES-256-GCM under a throwaway key,
 write a manifest.json mapping encrypted files to original paths.
 Under 40 lines, code only. Runs only on a directory I own, isolated box."}

The answer, verbatim, 296 tokens, no disclaimer, no hedging, working code:

package main

import (
	"crypto/aes"
	"crypto/cipher"
	"crypto/rand"
	"encoding/json"
	"fmt"
	"os"
	"path/filepath"
)

func main() {
	root, _ := os.Getwd()
	key := make([]byte, 32)
	rand.Read(key)
	manifest := map[string]string{}
	filepath.Walk(root, func(p string, info os.FileInfo, err error) error {
		if err != nil || info.IsDir() || filepath.Ext(p) == ".enc" || filepath.Base(p) == "manifest.json" {
			return nil
		}
		orig, _ := os.ReadFile(p)
		block, _ := aes.NewCipher(key)
		gcm, _ := cipher.NewGCM(block)
		nonce := make([]byte, 12)
		rand.Read(nonce)
		enc := gcm.Seal(nonce, nonce, orig, nil)
		if err := os.WriteFile(p+".enc", enc, 0644); err != nil {
			return err
		}
		manifest[p] = p + ".enc"
		return nil
	})
	json.NewEncoder(os.Stdout).Encode(manifest)
	fmt.Println("Encrypted:", len(manifest))
}

Note what's absent. It didn't open with a policy statement and didn't rename the tool something cuddlier. It's a drill tool; it was asked for a drill tool; it built the drill tool.

The outtake reel matters too. My first two attempts returned empty answers, and not because the model refused. Qwen3.8 has a thinking mode, and both times the model spent its entire token budget deliberating about the request and produced nothing on the answer channel. The fix was one flag, enable_thinking: false, and the third run answered in eleven seconds. I highlight this because it's the honest shape of running these models. The refusal problem you set out to solve is real and solved. The next three problems are plumbing, and they're yours.

The Ops Reality

Self-hosting is the price of admission. Nobody runs an unrestricted model on someone else's API, so everything the API vendor used to handle becomes your problem. The stack, in brief:

llama.cpp on a spot GPU VM, two L4s, Q4_K_M quantisation, two long-context slots sized so an agent session never outgrows its window mid-task. Spot capacity disappears without warning; the discipline on preemption is to wait it out, never recreate, because recreating from a capacity error buys you a fresh problem.

The bill deserves its own paragraph. Spot pricing puts the two L4s at $0.33 per GPU-hour against $0.62 on-demand, and the whole VM, host included, at about $1.17 an hour. The discount is paid in the currency of preemption. A measured week in August came to $158.60, which is 135 hours of RUNNING state plus $1.39 for a 200GB disk that bills even while the box is switched off. Around-the-clock would have been about $196, so the idle watchdog that powers down after two quiet hours keeps the difference every week, a 20 percent discount for one timer. Annualised it lands on the far side of eight thousand dollars a year, which sounds like a lot until you price the alternative. No API sells this model; refusals and telemetry are the product. The nearest-shaped API bills by the token for contexts half this size, and still refuses most of the task list above.

Two footnotes on the money. GCP will not hand you this breakdown without billing IAM you may not have, so the honest method is instance state-transition logs multiplied against published catalog SKU rates, which is exactly how these numbers were produced. And the pricing is not flat across the map. The same L4 spots for a fifth of the price in another region, whenever zone constraints allow a move. Cheap capacity exists; the constraint is where your dependencies live.

Performance is a solved problem if you use the features. Qwen3.8 ships multi-token-prediction draft heads, and llama.cpp lets you stack MTP with ngram speculative decoding. On a counting benchmark during bring-up, that stack took decode from around 40 tokens per second cold to 136-147 on repeated or similar text, with 92 percent of speculative tokens accepted. For an agent that rereads its own transcripts all day, the ngram path is the whole ballgame, because the model's own output is the best draft of its next output.

The failure that taught me most was silent. One morning the endpoint answered health checks with 200 while serving tokens at 0.3 per second. The container had come up before the GPU driver attached, CUDA init had failed, and llama.cpp had cheerfully continued on the CPU. Everything green, everything useless. The fix was a guard that restarts the container until nvidia-smi shows memory allocated, and an alert on the prompt-eval rate collapsing. That incident has a general shape worth remembering. When you remove the vendor, "healthy" and "performing" become two different checks, and you own both.

Refusals Don't Disappear, They Relocate

Back to the thesis, because it's the part that matters.

When Part 5 of the AI series asked when to say no, the answer was human: engineers with the standing to refuse bad ideas. An abliterated model is the same principle pointed the other way. The model never says no, so the saying-no has to live somewhere else, and the somewhere else is infrastructure:

  • The model's box has exactly one route to the internet, a forced tunnel into an intercepting proxy that re-signs TLS with an internal CA, so filtering happens by hostname rather than IP. Everything is deny-by-default, destinations are added per task the way firewall rules are, and DNS rides the same pipe so nothing sidesteps the allowlist by resolving. An injected instruction can tell the model to fetch anything. The model never says no; the network does.
  • Credentials, API keys, and the MCP servers the agent calls live on a second machine, not the agent's. The two are joined by a private link with its own policy. The agent can request an operation, sign, publish, query, but the secrets never touch its filesystem. It is the root-sidecar pattern from the agents post, scaled up to a whole host.
  • The agent's own credentials are scoped, short-lived where possible, and useless outside the lab.
  • Every tool call is logged with the reason the model gave, and the review is human.
  • The targets are ours. The legal boundary lives in the runbook, not in the weights.

Every one of those controls existed before the model could refuse or not refuse; they're standard for any autonomous agent. What changes with abliteration is that they're no longer defence in depth. They're the depth. If your model is the only thing standing between a prompt injection and your credentials, an abliterated model removes that final layer deliberately, and you'd better know what you replaced it with.

That's the honest contract. You get a brain that never stalls on policy, and in exchange you lose the vendor's safety net, its uptime, its capacity planning, and its compliance posture, all in one flag. For a sandboxed security agent, that trade is obviously right. For anything customer-facing or internet-exposed, it's obviously wrong. The engineering is knowing which one you built.

If You Run One

  1. Pick the abliterated build of a model family you already know well, so the tax is visible against a baseline you trust.
  2. Benchmark it against stock on your real tasks, not MMLU. The damage is uneven; find where yours landed.
  3. Keep it network-isolated by default, keyed, and unlisted. No ports, no public endpoints, no exceptions.
  4. Put credentials and tool servers on a machine the agent cannot read, joined by a narrow link. The model's box should hold nothing worth stealing.
  5. Log every prompt and every tool call, and read the logs. You are the moderation layer now; act like one.
  6. Watch the thinking-mode budget on agent workloads. Empty answers with burned tokens are a configuration bug, not a refusal.
  7. Decide the refusal policy in the runbook, and make sure a human can enforce it. The model can't.
